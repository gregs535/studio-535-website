Electrical Dispatcher AI Prompts for Safety-First Call Triage

Published August 5, 2026 by Studio 535 · Practical AI workflows for service businesses

An electrical dispatcher handles some of the highest-stakes calls in the trades: a burning smell, a tripping breaker, a flood near an outlet. AI can help with the writing and organization, but it must never give safety instructions, tell a customer to open a panel, or coach anyone to keep resetting a breaker.

The useful pattern is simple: collect the facts in a fixed intake form, ask AI to structure those facts, then have a dispatcher approve the result. That keeps the tool in its best role—drafting—while your team retains control of triage, promises, and customer communication.

The guardrail that matters most

If a caller reports smoke, sparks, a burning smell, water near electrical, or another immediate hazard, stop the normal AI workflow and follow your company’s emergency script. Direct the caller to emergency services when your policy requires it. Do not ask a language model to improvise safety instructions, and never instruct a customer to open a panel, touch wires, or repeatedly reset a breaker.

Start With a Dispatcher-Ready Intake

AI cannot repair missing source information. Before prompting, capture the customer’s name and callback number, service address, the customer’s own description of the issue, when it began, whether the problem affects one outlet, one circuit, multiple rooms, or the whole property, whether there is smoke or a burning smell, whether water is near any power source, the panel location and age if relevant, access notes, and available appointment windows.

Keep observations separate from conclusions. “Customer reports a breaker trips whenever the space heater runs” is useful. “The circuit is overloaded and the breaker is bad” is a diagnosis the dispatcher and AI are not in a position to make.

Copy-Paste Electrical Dispatch Prompt

This prompt converts rough notes into a concise summary without adding facts:

You are an administrative assistant for an electrical dispatch team.
Turn the notes below into a dispatch summary.

Return exactly these sections:
1. Customer and location
2. Reported issue (use the customer’s words; do not diagnose)
3. Timing and observable symptoms (trip pattern, scope, flicker)
4. Safety signals (smoke, sparks, burning smell, water near power)
5. Panel and access details
6. Missing information the dispatcher should ask for
7. One customer confirmation text under 320 characters

Rules:
- Use only the supplied facts.
- Never invent an arrival time, price, warranty status, cause, or repair.
- Flag safety keywords for immediate human review.
- Never instruct the customer to open a panel or touch wiring.
- If a detail is unknown, write “not provided.”

CALL NOTES:
[PASTE DE-IDENTIFIED OR COMPANY-APPROVED NOTES]

Five High-Value Dispatcher Uses

1. Tripping-breaker triage notes

Ask AI to organize breaker calls into a standard brief: which breaker, how often it trips, what loads are on the circuit, and whether the panel feels warm. Require the model to mark the customer’s actions verbatim and to add a “do not keep resetting” note to the confirmation draft.

2. Outage versus property problem

Before promising a truck, ask AI to draft the utility-outage check: whole-house or partial, neighbors affected, and what the utility map shows. The dispatcher confirms the map; the model never decides that a visit is unnecessary.

3. GFCI coaching drafts

For single dead outlets, provide the model with your approved GFCI-check script and ask it to draft a message that invites the customer to press RESET on nearby GFCI outlets. Keep the draft within your approved procedure, and route any repeat trips straight to dispatch.

4. Appointment confirmation drafts

Provide the approved date, service window, address, access instructions (including panel location), and office callback number. Tell the model to repeat those details exactly and avoid language such as “the electrician will fix it today.” The message should confirm the visit, not promise an outcome.

5. Estimate and inspection follow-ups

Draft day-2 and day-5 follow-ups using only the quoted scope, price, and validity window, plus any permit/inspection status. Ask for a polite check-in that offers to walk through options without inventing discounts.

Use a Five-Point Review Before Sending

  1. Facts: Compare names, phone numbers, addresses, dates, windows, breaker labels, and quoted prices against the source record.
  2. Promises: Remove invented arrival times, diagnoses, parts availability, coverage decisions, and repair guarantees.
  3. Safety: Route hazard language and uncertain urgency to a trained human using the company’s approved procedure.
  4. Tone: Make the message direct, calm, and easy to act on.
  5. Privacy: Use only tools and data-handling practices approved by your business. Minimize customer information whenever possible.

A Better Prompt Formula for Any Electrical Office

Build each prompt from five parts: role (administrative dispatch assistant), source facts (your approved intake fields), output (summary, text, or checklist), boundaries (no diagnosis, no safety instructions, no invented promises), and review rule (a dispatcher approves before use). This formula is more dependable than asking, “Write a reply to this customer.”
