How Makers Use AI to Write Etsy Listings That Actually Sell

Published by Studio 535 · Precision Crafted. Technology Engineered.

You make the thing. The walnut board, the laser-engraved coaster, the custom sign. Then you sit down to write the listing and the cursor blinks. Most makers either skip the description, paste something generic, or burn an hour rewriting the same three sentences. That hour, multiplied across every product, is real money left on the table.

AI tools — the free tiers of ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini — can turn a 60-minute listing into a 10-minute one. But only if you prompt them right. Vague prompts get vague mush that sounds like every other listing on Etsy. Here is the system we use in our own shop.

The 5-Part Listing Structure

Every high-converting maker listing answers the same five questions. Build your prompt around them:

  • Who is this for? Name the buyer. "A daughter buying a Father's Day gift" writes itself differently than "a woodworker."
  • What feeling does owning it create? Benefit language, not feature language.
  • What are the hard facts? Materials, dimensions, finish, care. The AI cannot know these — you must supply them.
  • What's the format? Hook sentence, two short paragraphs, bullet spec list. Under 180 words.
  • What's banned? Kill the AI-isms: "stunning," "elevate," "perfect for any occasion."

The Copy-Paste Prompt

You are a copywriter for handmade goods on Etsy. My buyer is [DESCRIBE BUYER].
Write a product description for [PRODUCT + SPECS + MATERIALS + PROCESS].
Structure: (1) one-sentence hook about the feeling, (2) materials/craftsmanship
paragraph, (3) customization paragraph, (4) bulleted spec list, (5) care + shipping.
Under 180 words. Warm but not flowery. Never use: stunning, elevate, beautiful, perfect.

Why This Works

Naming the buyer forces benefit language. The banned-words list kills the robot-speak that makes listings look mass-produced. The hard limit ("under 180 words") stops the AI from padding. One prompt, ten minutes of polish, a listing that reads like a $200 brand.

Chain It

One listing draft becomes fuel for everything else: run the same facts through a title-and-tags prompt, then a social-caption prompt, then a customer-email template. You get a week of marketing from a single product description.
